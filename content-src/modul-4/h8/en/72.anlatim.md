### A button is a mechanical part
Inside a real button there are metal contacts. When you press, these contacts hit each other and chatter for a very short time. On a single press the pin goes back and forth between HIGH and LOW several times. This is called **bounce**. Bounce usually lasts milliseconds, but because the processor works in microseconds, it sees each one as a separate press.

The button part in the lab does not do this: the pin goes LOW in one move. That is why we imitated bounce in code in the lesson. In the imitation, you saw one press and release as 5 counts in total.

### Debouncing: wait for it to stay calm
The fix is to wait for the pin to **stay the same for a certain time**:

```
on every change: "time of last change" = now
if now - last change >= SAKIN_SURE:
    accept the value
```

That way the bounce sequence is swallowed within the waiting time. If `SAKIN_SURE` = 20 ms, a press released before 20 ms are up is never counted. This is the cost of the waiting time. If you choose it too short, bounce leaks through; if you choose it too long, you miss fast presses. Also, do not forget the bounce that happens on release: the wait must work in both directions.

### The pin is not read during delay()
While `delay(500)` runs, the program does nothing, and does not look at the pin either. The loop works like this: read the pin, wait 500 ms, read the pin. If you press the button briefly and release it between these two readings, the program never sees the press. Timing effects are real in the lab, because the code runs at its real speed. So you miss the press in the lab too.

The fix is to use `millis()`: the slow job keeps its own time, and `loop()` looks at the pin on every pass. A pass takes a few microseconds, so the press is seen straight away.

### Sensors do not answer instantly
Sensors have their own timings: the DHT needs one reading every two seconds, and the I2C LCD about one second at start-up. The lab models these; if you forget the wait in your code, the lab catches you too. PIR warm-up is modelled as well, but it is shortened (2 s in the lab, much longer in reality).

### Summary
Real buttons bounce; the lab button does not. Debouncing swallows bounce by waiting for the pin to stay calm for a certain time; the cost is the risk of missing very short presses. The pin is not read during `delay()`, while `millis()` makes the response faster. The sentence "there was no bounce in the lab" does not show that debouncing is unnecessary.
