### The lab is a clean world
The lab does not imitate every side of a real circuit. This week we set a rule: you say "I saw it in the lab" only if the lab really produces an effect. If it does not, you know this clearly and imitate the effect in code.

| Effect | In the lab |
|---|---|
| ADC step (1024 levels) | Modelled |
| Timing (`delay`, `millis`) | Modelled |
| Analogue noise | Not modelled |
| Drift of the reference voltage | Not modelled |
| Button bounce | Not modelled |

### The ADC step
`analogRead` divides the 0-5 V range into 1023 equal parts. One **step** is 5 V / 1023 ≈ 4.89 mV. Voltages in between are rounded to the nearest step. So at best, the ADC sees voltage in steps of 4.89 mV. For example, a voltage of 2.550 V is rounded to step 522, and converting it back gives 2551.3 mV. This 1.3 mV difference is not an error; it is rounding.

### Noise
**Noise** is the small, random wobbles that get mixed into the sensor voltage without anyone wanting them. Wires behave like aerials, the power line ripples, and the ADC itself jitters by a step or two. The lab's voltage, however, is perfectly steady, so when you leave the potentiometer still, the value does not move at all. In a real circuit it does.

You cannot remove noise, but you can shrink it: the average of N readings reduces the noise about square-root(N) times. 4 times with 16 readings, 8 times with 64 readings. The cost is time: 64 readings mean 64 times more work than 1 reading.

### Noise makes a threshold flicker
If you decide with a single threshold (`deger > 512`), while the value is near the threshold the decision changes on every wave of noise and the LED flashes on and off. The fix is two thresholds:

```
to switch on:   value > 524
to switch off:  value < 500
in between:     the previous decision is kept
```

The gap in between (24 here) must be bigger than the size of the noise. If the noise is ±8, a gap narrower than ±8 brings the flicker back.

### Summary
The lab really models the ADC step and timing, but not noise, button bounce or reference drift. A reading is at most as accurate as one step. Against noise, you use averaging and a two-threshold decision (hysteresis). The sentence "it did not flicker in the lab" is not proof that it will not flicker in a real circuit.
