### Sound measures distance
When you shout "hey!" in the mountains, the echo comes back a little later. The later the echo returns, the farther away the mountain is. The **HC-SR04** does the same job with a sound too high for people to hear (**ultrasonic**, 40 kHz on this sensor). It has two cylinders on it: one sends the sound (T), the other listens (R).

### Four pins, two jobs
| Pin | Job |
|---|---|
| VCC, GND | The sensor's 5 V supply |
| TRIG | From the board to the sensor: "measure now" (a short HIGH pulse) |
| ECHO | From the sensor to the board: HIGH until the echo arrives |

The sensor does not tell you centimetres. The ECHO pin stays HIGH from the moment the sound goes out until the echo returns. You work out the distance from this **time**.

### The formula
Sound travels through air at about 343 m/s. Let's convert that to cm/µs:

```
343 m/s = 34 300 cm/s = 0.0343 cm/µs
distance (cm) = time (µs) × 0.0343 / 2
```

Why divide by two? The time you measure is the **there-and-back** time, while the distance is only the way there. Example: if ECHO stayed HIGH for 2000 µs, 2000 × 0.0343 = 68.6; divided by two that is 34.3 cm. Shortcut: 1 cm takes about 58 µs there and back; dividing the time by 58 also gives you centimetres.

### Not everything is 343 m/s
The speed of sound changes with temperature. In dry air it is roughly:

```
c ≈ 331.3 + 0.606 × T      (c: m/s, T: °C)
```

At 0 °C, c = 331.3 m/s; at 40 °C it is 355.5 m/s. If your program always assumes 343 m/s, in the cold you find the distance **larger** than it is, and in the heat **smaller**. A real 100 cm looks like about 103.5 cm at 0 °C and about 96.5 cm at 40 °C. For a parking sensor this difference is usually acceptable; if you need centimetre accuracy, you measure the temperature and put it into the formula.

### Limits
The HC-SR04's typical range is 2 cm to 400 cm. At 400 cm the sound travels 8 metres, which takes about 23 ms. Outside this range, or when the sound scatters in a way that does not return to the sensor, there is no valid measurement.

### Summary
The HC-SR04 gives the there-and-back time of the sound as the time the ECHO pin stays HIGH. The distance is found with distance = time × 0.0343 / 2; we divide by two because the sound covers the path twice. Since the speed of sound depends on temperature, assuming a fixed 343 m/s can cause a drift of a few centimetres.
