### What you will learn in this lesson
- What voltage, current and resistance are, and their units
- Ohm's law (V = I × R) and how to calculate with it
- Why an LED needs a series resistor and how to choose its value
- How to read the current with virtual measurement in the lab

### What you should know first
The idea of HIGH (≈ 5 V) and LOW (0 V) from lesson 1. The four operations and decimal numbers are enough.

### Three basic quantities
You can think of an electric circuit as a **water pipe**. This comparison is not real, but it helps the idea sink in.

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voltage | V | volt (V) | The pressure that pushes the water |
| Current | I | ampere (A) | The amount of water flowing through the pipe |
| Resistance | R | ohm (Ω) | How narrow the pipe is |

We write small currents in **milliamperes** (mA): 1 A = 1000 mA. We write large resistances in **kilohms** (kΩ): 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω.

### Ohm's law
The voltage across a resistor is equal to the current through it multiplied by the resistance:

**V = I × R**

You use it in three forms: `I = V / R` (find the current), `R = V / I` (find the resistance), `V = I × R` (find the voltage). Before calculating, **convert the units**: write the current in amperes and the resistance in ohms.

**Example.** The current through a 1 kΩ resistor connected to 5 V: I = 5 / 1000 = 0.005 A = **5 mA**. If you make the resistor 500 Ω, the current becomes **10 mA**. The resistance was halved, and the current doubled.

### Why do we put a resistor on an LED?
Unlike a resistor, an LED does not limit the current by itself. While it is lit, an almost constant voltage drops across it (about 2 V for red). The remaining voltage has to drop somewhere. The **series resistor** in between takes that remaining voltage and sets the current:

`I = (V besleme − V LED) / R`

Here `V besleme` is the supply voltage. For a 5 V supply, a 2 V red LED and 330 Ω: I = (5 − 2) / 330 ≈ 0.009 A ≈ **9 mA**. This is a current at which the LED lights safely. If you had used 100 Ω, the current would be 30 mA; that is too much.

### Calculation and measurement
This calculation is idealised. In reality (and in the lab), the Uno pin's internal resistance and the LED's internal resistance also take a share of the voltage. That is why the measured current comes out a little smaller than the calculation: about 8.6 mA at 330 Ω. The small difference is normal and is not a mistake; it shows that the model is more detailed.

### Summary
Voltage is the push, current is the flow, resistance is the obstacle. Ohm's law connects all three: V = I × R. The resistor sets the LED's current; that is why we always put a series resistor on an LED. In this module we use **330 Ω** for a red LED at 5 V.
