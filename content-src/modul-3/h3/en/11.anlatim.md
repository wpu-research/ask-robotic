### Analog and digital: two classes, infinitely many values
`digitalRead` puts the voltage on a pin into one of two classes: HIGH or LOW. But in the real world the voltage can take any value between 0 V and 5 V. This continuously changing signal is called **analog**. Sensors usually report light, temperature or the position of a knob with a voltage like this.

### The ADC: a ruler that turns voltage into a number
The **ADC** inside the Uno measures the 0-5 V range with 10 bits. 10 bits means 2 to the power of 10 = **1024** different numbers (0-1023). The width of one step is 5 V / 1024 ≈ 4.9 mV. Think of a ruler: a length of 5 V divided into 1024 notches, and the ADC tells you which notch the voltage falls on.

```
voltage = ham * 5.0 / 1024.0
```

| analogRead | Approximate voltage |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 V |
| 256 | 1.25 V |
| 512 | 2.5 V |
| 1023 | ≈ 5 V |

For `analogRead(A0)` you do not write `pinMode`; analog pins are already inputs.

### The potentiometer: a voltage you set by hand
The two outer terminals of the potentiometer are connected to 5V and GND; the middle terminal (SIG) moves between 0-5 V as the slider turns. If you connect SIG to A0, you read the slider's position as a number. If the position is 0.5, the number is about 512.

### Decimal maths and scaling
If you write `ham * 5 / 1024`, both sides are whole numbers; the result comes out as 0, 1, 2 with everything after the decimal point thrown away. That is why you write `5.0`. `analogWrite`, on the other hand, wants 0-255. Since 1024 / 4 = 256, the result of `ham / 4` is in the range 0-255.

```
float volt = ham * 5.0 / 1024.0;   // correct
int yari = ham / 4;                // 0-255
```

### Summary
The ADC turns a voltage into a number from 0 to 1023; one step is about 4.9 mV. To go back from the number to a voltage, you calculate `ham × 5,0 / 1024`, and `5.0` must be written as a decimal. A potentiometer behaves like a voltage source you set by hand. `ham / 4` moves the 0-1023 range into the 0-255 range of `analogWrite`.
