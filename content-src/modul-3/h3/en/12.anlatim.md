### The voltage divider
Let the top resistor R1 be connected to 5 V and the bottom resistor R2 to GND. The current is the same through both resistors: I = Vin / (R1 + R2). The voltage at the middle point is the voltage across R2:

```
Vout = Vin * R2 / (R1 + R2)
```

With two equal resistors, Vout is exactly in the middle: 2.5 V. Example: if R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 = 30 kΩ, then Vout = 5 × 30 / 40 = 3.75 V. As the bottom resistor gets bigger, Vout rises.

### Why a divider?
An Arduino cannot measure a part's resistance directly; it only measures the voltage on the pin. If we connect a sensor whose resistance changes in series with a fixed resistor, the change in resistance turns into a change in voltage at the middle point. The ADC reads this.

### LDR
An **LDR** is a resistor whose resistance goes down as the light increases. In the dark it is very large (hundreds of kΩ), and in bright light it is very small. If you put the LDR on top and the fixed 10 kΩ resistor at the bottom: in the light the LDR gets smaller, and Vout and `ham` rise; in the dark they fall.

You can also calculate in the other direction. With the LDR on top, from the `ham` value read on A1:

```
R_ldr = 10000.0 * (1024 - ham) / ham
```

Example: if ham = 256, then R_ldr = 10000 × 768 / 256 = 30 000 Ω.

### Hysteresis
When the light is exactly at the threshold value, `ham` wobbles around like 249, 251, 250; an LED with a single threshold keeps blinking on and off. The solution is two thresholds: turn it on below 250, turn it off above 350. Between the two, do nothing; the LED keeps its previous state.

### Summary
A voltage divider turns a change in resistance into a voltage you can measure: Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2). With the LDR on top and the fixed resistor at the bottom, `ham` rises as the light increases. To find the resistance again, the formula is turned around. Hysteresis prevents flickering near the threshold by using two thresholds.
