### What you will learn in this lesson
- The rule BPE (byte pair encoding) uses to learn pieces
- How pairs are counted in one merge step
- How learned merges are applied to new text
- Why a word that has never been seen can still be split into pieces

### What you should know first
Lesson 2: tokens, number of merges and corpus. The tool again runs in the browser with real calculations.

### Who wrote the vocabulary?
Nobody told the tokeniser "make robot a single piece". The pieces appear on their own by repeating a single rule:

1. Split every word in the corpus into its letters.
2. Count all the pairs that sit next to each other; find the most common pair.
3. Make that pair a single new piece, and write this **merge** in the list, in order.
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until you reach the number you want.

### A real example
These are the first merges learned from the small Turkish corpus in the tool (with the start-of-word mark):

| Order | Merge | The word "robot" |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | (none) | ▁ r o b o t (6 tokens) |
| 2 | r + o | ▁ ro b o t (5) |
| 3 | o + t | ▁ ro b ot (4) |
| 4 | b + ot | ▁ ro bot (3) |
| 5 | ro + bot | ▁ robot (2) |
| 7 | ▁ + robot | ▁robot (1) |

Because "robot" appears a lot in the corpus, its pieces merged step by step. If a pair appears only once in the corpus during counting, the tool does not merge it; it must be seen at least twice.

### Applying it to new text
When training is over, what you have is a **merge list**. When you tokenise a new text, you first split it into letters, then apply the merges in the list in the order they were learned. The order matters: merge 3 can only become a meaningful chain after merge 2 has been done. The slider in the tool chooses how many of the first steps in the list are applied. For `robot sağa döner ve ileri gider` (the robot turns right and goes forward) the result changes like this:

| Merges applied | Tokens | Characters | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 32 | 26 | 1.23 |
| 20 | 14 | 26 | 0.54 |
| 40 | 11 | 26 | 0.42 |

### A word that has never been seen
For the word `kalemlik` (pencil case), which does not appear in the corpus, most merges are no use and the word breaks into small pieces; in the worst case it stays letter by letter. This is a safety net: the model can represent even a word it has never seen through its pieces. In real systems the process starts at byte level instead of letter level, so no text is ever "unknown".

### Summary
BPE learns pieces by merging the pair that sits next to each other most often in the corpus, again and again. The result is a merge list in which the order matters. Common words become a single token; rare words are split up. A word that has never been seen is still represented, in the worst case by being split into small pieces.
