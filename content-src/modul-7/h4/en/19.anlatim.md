### What you will learn in this lesson
- The ideas of edge cases and threshold values
- How to test the limits of a rule
- How to catch an input that is not taken into account with paired cases
- What data leakage is and how it is prevented

### What you should know first
Lessons 16-18: evals, writing cases, accuracy and recall. You also need to know the difference between "less than" (<) and "less than or equal to" (<=).

### Mistakes hide at the limits
Think of a rule like this for a robot: if the distance is less than 20 cm, "stop"; if it is less than 50 cm, "slow down"; otherwise, "continue". The numbers 20 and 50, where the rule changes its decision, are called **thresholds**. The inputs right next to a threshold are called **edge cases**: 19, 20, 49, 50. When you write code, it is very easy to write `<=` instead of `<`, and this mistake only appears at exactly the threshold value. Someone who tries 5 cm or 35 cm never sees it.

### The exact behaviour of the rule
For this rule, the decisions for whole-number distances from 0 to 60 are:

```
0 ... 19    -> stop        (20 values)
20 ... 49   -> slow down   (30 values)
50 ... 60   -> continue    (11 values)
```

"Shifting" a threshold towards one side changes the rule: a rule that says "stop" at 20 cm does not meet the specification "stop if it is less than 20". That is why you write both sides of the threshold as cases.

### An input that is not taken into account
This week's robot rule also sees the speed in its input, but it looks only at the distance. This is a deliberate gap: if the distance is 40 cm and the robot is going at 100 cm per second, "slow down" may not be enough. How do you notice this? By writing **paired cases**: two cases where only the speed changes (`mesafe=35 hız=10` and `mesafe=35 hız=90`, distance 35 with speed 10 and with speed 90). As a person you expect different answers for these two; if the rule gives the same answer, you prove that the speed is not taken into account. If you change more than one input at a time, you cannot tell which one had the effect.

### Data leakage
If, while improving the rule, you always look at the same 6 cases and then measure success with the same 6 cases, the result may come out at 100%. That is because you tuned the rule to those cases. This is called **data leakage**, and it makes the measurement look better than it really is. The solution: keep one set for tuning and a separate set for the final measurement.

### Summary
Mistakes hide right on both sides of the thresholds; that is why edge cases are the most valuable part of a test set. Inputs that are not taken into account are caught with paired cases in which only that input changes. Keeping the tuning set and the measurement set apart stops the result from coming out too optimistic.
