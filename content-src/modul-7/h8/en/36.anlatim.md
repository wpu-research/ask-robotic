### What you will learn in this lesson
- What a hallucination is, and why it can be wrong even though it is written fluently
- How to compare a claim with the source and sort it into one of three types: matches, contradicts, not in the source
- How to express the hallucination rate as a number
- The methods that reduce the risk, and why they do not remove it

### What you should know first
It is enough to know that a language model chooses the next piece by probability (lessons 4 and 6), that RAG puts a source into the context (lessons 23-24), and that an eval tests a system with cases (lessons 16-20).

### What is a hallucination?
A **hallucination** is when a model produces information that looks fluent and reasonable but has no source, or is wrong. The model does not "decide to lie". It only writes, one after another, the words that look likely at that point. If the information is not in the context, the gap can be filled with a guess that sounds right. So an answer being well written does not show that it is right.

### Three types of mistake
If you compare a summary with the source line by line, every claim falls into one of these three groups:

```
Source: "The club meets on Tuesdays."
Claim                                  Result
The club meets on Tuesdays.            matches
The club meets on Mondays.             contradicts
The club serves pizza on Tuesdays.     not in the source (made-up detail)
```

The third type is the sneakiest, because it does not contradict the source, it sounds sensible, and you only catch it by looking at the source. There is also **exaggeration**: the source says "usually" but the summary says "always".

### Measure the mistakes with numbers
The sentence "This model hallucinates a lot" is not a measurement. Collect cases as in an eval and work out the rate. Example: 50 summaries were checked, and 4 of them had at least one made-up detail. The hallucination rate is 4 / 50 = 8%, and the rate of summaries without hallucination is 92%. What matters is to write down, next to the number, which 50 summaries you looked at; with different sources the rate can change.

### Reducing the risk
- Put the source into the context (RAG) and say "rely only on the source".
- Give the instruction "If you do not know, say you do not know".
- Use tools for calculations and up-to-date information; do not trust the model's memory.
- For important claims, have it cite a source, then **open the source yourself and check it**.
- Measure the output regularly with an eval.

None of these removes the risk. That is why, for important decisions, the final check always stays with a human.

### What is real on this portal, and what is not?
The summaries in the Hallucination detective are **hand-written examples**; they are not a real model's output. What is real is that your marks are compared with the source and scored.

### Summary
A hallucination is output that has no source but looks convincing. Compare every claim with the source and sort it into matches, contradicts or not in the source. Count the mistakes over cases and turn them into a rate. Giving sources, using tools and evals reduce the risk but do not remove it; checking an important claim against its source is still up to you.
