### What you will learn in this lesson
- How a language model chooses the next word using **probabilities**
- How **temperature** sharpens or flattens the probability distribution
- How to solve a small temperature calculation by hand
- That a low temperature does not mean "correct"

### What you should know first
From lesson 4, that next-word prediction is done by counting and that every candidate has a count; from lesson 5, generating text with your mini language model. The idea of tokens from lesson 2 will also help.

### The model produces a list of probabilities, not a single answer
At every step the model ranks the candidates and gives each one a probability. Then it **chooses one** from this list. The choice is a bit like a prize draw: a candidate with a high probability comes up often, but a candidate with a low probability also comes up sometimes. This is why you get two different answers to the same question.

The mini model in the Workshop shows this clearly. In the corpus, 12 different words came after `robot`; the total count is 22. `engeli` (the obstacle) appears 3 times, so its probability is 3/22, about 0.136. `yavaş` (slowly) appears only once, so it is 1/22, about 0.045.

### What does temperature do?
**Temperature** is a setting used when the probabilities are worked out. The rule is simple: the logarithm of each candidate's count is divided by the temperature, then turned into a probability with **softmax**. The same thing can also be written like this: the probability is proportional to the count raised to the power 1/T.

Example by hand: there are two candidates with counts 4 and 1.
```
T = 1   ->  4 and 1      ->  0.80 and 0.20
T = 0.5 ->  16 and 1     ->  0.94 and 0.06  (sharpens)
T = 2   ->  2 and 1      ->  0.67 and 0.33  (flattens)
```
When the temperature gets smaller, the first candidate becomes even more dominant; when it gets bigger, the two candidates move closer together. At a very large temperature the probabilities become almost equal (they head towards 0.5 and 0.5).

### Temperature 0 and uncertainty
When the temperature gets very close to 0, the model always chooses the candidate with the highest count; this is called **greedy selection**. In the Workshop, if several candidates have equal counts (for example after `robot`: `engeli`, `çizgiyi` and `duvarı`, the obstacle, the line and the wall, all have 3), the tie is broken by alphabetical order and the first one is chosen. That is why different seeds give the same text.

**Uncertainty** (entropy) measures how spread out the probabilities are. For the 12 candidates after `robot`, the highest possible value is about 3.58 bits; at temperature 0.3 it is about 2.7 bits, and at temperature 1 about 3.45 bits.

### Which temperature for which job?
- Data extraction, classification, code: low temperature, consistent results.
- Poems, finding names, brainstorming: high temperature, variety.
- A low temperature is **not a guarantee of correctness**: the model can say a wrong answer in the same way every time too.

Note: the model you see in this lesson is not a neural network, but a small table made from counts; in real services temperature works with the same idea, but its values and names vary from service to service.

### Summary
The model ranks the candidates by probability and chooses one. Temperature sharpens or flattens these probabilities: when it is low, the output is consistent and predictable; when it is high, it is varied but scattered. When the temperature is close to 0 the most likely candidate is always chosen, but that does not show the answer is correct.
