### What is odometry?
If you count "5 steps forward, turn left, 3 steps" while walking with your eyes closed, you roughly know where you are. **Odometry** is the robot working out its own position (x, y) and heading (θ) from its wheel turns. At the start, x = y = θ = 0 is taken; θ = 0 means the robot faces the +x direction, and a positive angle is a left turn.

### Three formulas
In a small time step, the left wheel travelled ΔsL and the right wheel ΔsR (number of ticks times tick distance). The distance between the wheels is L = 12 cm:

```
Δs  = (ΔsL + ΔsR) / 2          distance travelled by the centre
Δθ  = (ΔsR − ΔsL) / L          turn (left positive)
x  += Δs · cos(θ + Δθ/2)
y  += Δs · sin(θ + Δθ/2)
θ  += Δθ
```

Because the heading also changes during the step, the heading in the middle of the step (θ + Δθ/2) is used for the position. You call this calculation often, inside `loop()` and while waiting; if the steps stay small, the estimate is good. `cos` and `sin` want radians; do not call them with degrees.

Our encoder does not tell you the direction; to count the ticks of a wheel driven backwards as negative, the last direction given is known from the command.

### Source of error 1: tick resolution
One tick is about 1.04 cm of travel; with a 12 cm axle track this means about 5° of turning uncertainty. While the robot is trying to turn 90°, odometry sees 89°, but it may really have turned 94°. If you walk 1 m with 5° of error, you drift about 9 cm sideways. The solution: set the interrupt to `CHANGE` and count two edges for each slot; the number of ticks becomes 40, the tick distance drops to 0.52 cm, and in the simulation the difference falls to 0.5 cm.

### Source of error 2: wrong constants
Even if the resolution is increased, the estimate stays wrong if the numbers in the formula are wrong. If the radius is really 3.45 cm instead of 3.3, or the axle track 12.5 cm instead of 12, the estimate is off by about 14 cm over a 2.5 m path. The program cannot see this, because the ticks have been counted correctly. This **systematic error** grows in proportion as the path gets longer: with the axle track 4% wrong, about 16° of drift builds up on every full turn.

### Worse on a real robot
In the simulation the wheel does not slip. On a real robot, slipping, sloping floors, collisions and wear come in too. Odometry never corrects itself; it needs to be supported by another sensor (for example a gyroscope).

### Summary
Odometry works out x, y and θ step by step from the wheel distances: the average distance moves the robot forward, and the distance difference divided by L turns it. The error comes from two places: tick resolution (halved with CHANGE) and a wrong radius or axle track (permanent). Both errors build up along the path, and odometry cannot see its own error.
