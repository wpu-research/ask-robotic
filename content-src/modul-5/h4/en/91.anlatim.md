### Looking at turns, not time
Until now you told the robot "go for 2 seconds" and it covered roughly some distance. When the battery gets weak or the floor gets slippery, the same two seconds give a different distance. A better way is to count how many times the wheel turns. The part that does this is the **encoder**.

### Disc, slot and tick
An encoder is a thin disc with slots around its edge that turns together with the wheel. On one side of the disc there is a light source, and on the other a light sensor. When a slot passes, the light is visible; when a solid part of the disc passes, it is blocked. The sensor's output rises from LOW to HIGH at each pass. We count only this **RISING** edge, and we call each count a **tick**.

Our disc has 20 slots, so when the wheel turns once, 20 ticks come in.

### From ticks to distance
When the wheel turns once, it travels its own circumference (we assume it does not slip). For a radius r = 3.3 cm:

```
circumference = 2 · π · r              = 20.73 cm
tick distance = circumference / slots = 20.73 / 20 = 1.037 cm
distance      = number of ticks · 1.037 cm
```

The reverse works too: target distance / 1.037 tells us how many ticks are needed. For 1 metre this is 96.5 ticks. Because the counter is a whole number, the target is rounded and the distance is measured in steps of about 1 cm.

### Not missing ticks
A slot may pass while the robot is busy with something else. That is why an interrupt pin is used for each wheel (D2 and D3). The interrupt function only increases the counter and does nothing else. Because the counter changes inside the interrupt, it must be `volatile`; otherwise the compiler may use an old value inside `loop()`. Because the counter is an `unsigned int` (2 bytes), the reading is placed between `noInterrupts()` and `interrupts()` so that no interrupt comes in while it is being read.

### The counter tells the truth, not your intention
When you tell the robot to stop at 97 ticks, the motor does not stop instantly; it keeps turning for a few tens of milliseconds and the counter goes up to 98. The counter gives the distance the robot **really travelled**. That is why the measured value is often a little more than requested.

### Summary
An encoder counts each slot passing on the wheel as a tick. The tick distance is the circumference divided by the number of slots; on a 20-slot wheel with a radius of 3.3 cm it comes to 1.037 cm. Ticks are counted with an interrupt, the counter is `volatile` and it is read safely. The counter shows the distance the robot really covered, not the distance you wanted.
