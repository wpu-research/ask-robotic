### The three documents of the presentation
When someone asks "So how good is it?" and you say "it works well", the conversation ends. If you say "its criterion is 20 ± 3 cm, and I measured 18.3 to 22.0 cm", the conversation begins. Three documents are needed: a **results table**, a **bug report** and a **daily report**. They are all plain text; what matters is not fancy slides but numbers you can trust.

### Results table
Four columns for each criterion: what was asked for, how it was measured, what was measured, the result. Three things keep it honest: the "how it was measured" column (a number without a method is not proof), a note on the scope, and where the result came from. For example, the 0.09 mm error for the arm is the commanded position in the simulator; gear backlash, bending and the servo's dead band were not measured. That is why the row says "PASSED (limited scope)".

### The limit of measuring
The robot can measure its own score (time, collisions, average line error), but it is honest only to the resolution of the device it measures with. The line sensors are placed 20 mm apart; a sensor that sees the line counts the error as 0, while the line may be within ±10 mm of it. That is why the score is reported not as "the real deviation" but as "the deviation seen by the sensor array". In the collision counter, too, presses closer together than 500 ms are merged, so that the vibration of the same bumper counts as one.

### Root cause analysis
The **symptom** is what you see ("the robot got stuck at the end of the wall"); the **root cause** is the first layer that produces the symptom. The report contains: the symptom, reproducing it, a layer check (what was measured in each layer, is it sound), the root cause, a **single** change, verification (the same test and neighbouring tests) and the lesson learned.

Example: with `YAN_MS = 1500`, the robot was 6 mm short of the end of the wall: the required y ≥ 1.15 + 0.075 = 1.225 m, the y reached was 1.219 m. The turn, the decision and the sensor were sound; the root cause was the sideways time. The single change was `YAN_MS: 1500 -> 2000`. Bugs at the limit, the ones described as "it sometimes fixes itself", come from narrow margins like this.

### Daily report and commit
Daily report: goal, what I did, measurements (number + unit + how), problem-root cause-fix, the reason for the decision, tomorrow. The goal: someone reading it six months later should be able to find the answer to "why this value?". A commit message is a mini report too: not "fixed it", but "YAN_MS 1500 -> 2000, the robot was getting stuck because it was 6 mm short".

### Summary
A trustworthy presentation comes with numbers, a measurement method and a scope limit. A bug report separates the symptom from the root cause, measures every layer, makes a single change and runs the neighbouring tests too. A scoreboard is honest only to the resolution of the device it measures with. Simulator results are not presented as real robot results.
