- Why the outer loop must run slower than the inner loop
- Why limiting the target angle and choosing the right signs matter

### Why is one loop not enough?
The angle PD's target is always 0°: `komut = KP · açı + KD · açıHızı` (command = KP · angle + KD · angle speed). But the point the sensor calls "0°" may not be the robot's real balance point; the sensor may be mounted crookedly, or the centre of mass may be in front. The robot stands slightly tilted and has to keep driving in the same direction to stay upright. The result is **drift**: the robot does not fall, but slowly walks away. In the simulation, a 4° sensor offset meant a drift of about 16.6 cm in 15 seconds.

### Cascade: two controllers stacked
| | Outer loop | Inner loop |
|---|---|---|
| Input | Position and speed | Angle and angle speed |
| Output | Target angle | Motor command |
| Speed | Slow (20 ms) | Fast (5 ms) |

The output of the outer loop is the target of the inner loop: `komut = KP · (açı − hedefAçı) + KD · açıHızı` (with `hedefAçı`, the target angle). If the robot is going forwards, the outer loop makes the target angle **negative**; to bring the robot to that angle, the inner loop first drives forwards, the robot leans back and comes back. That is why the position and speed gains (`KX`, `KV`) are negative. The wrong sign does not correct the drift; it speeds it up.

### Why must the outer loop be slow?
It takes time for the inner loop to settle at a new target angle. If the outer loop changes the target every round, the inner loop cannot keep up and the two chase each other. Rule: make the outer loop about 5-10 times slower than the inner loop. In this course we use 5 ms and 20 ms (4 times). A far too slow outer loop also reacts late; in the simulation, at 8 times slower, the oscillation grew again.

### Limit the target angle
The outer loop's target angle was limited to `±6°`. Without a limit, a large position error forces the robot to lean further than the inner loop can recover from. Example: if `KX = −60`, `KV = −150`, the position is 0.05 m and the speed 0.04 m/s, the target comes out as −3 − 6 = −9°; the limit brings it down to −6°.

### Summary
An angle PD alone keeps the robot upright, but it drifts at a wrongly calibrated balance point. In cascade control, a slow outer loop looks at position and speed and gives the inner loop a target angle. The outer loop must run slowly, its output must be limited and its gains must have the right signs. The gain numbers belong to the robot in the simulation; on a real robot they are tuned again.
