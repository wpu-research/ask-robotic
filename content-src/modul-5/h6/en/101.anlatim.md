### Why measure tilt?
A balancing robot is like keeping a ruler upright on your finger. To notice that it is starting to fall, you first need to know the **tilt**, that is, how many degrees the robot is leaning from the vertical. This week, leaning forwards counts as positive.

### Accelerometer: it looks at gravity
A still sensor feels only gravity (1 g). As the robot leans, this 1 g is shared between the sensor's X and Z axes:

| Tilt θ | ax (g) | az (g) |
|---|---|---|
| 0° | 0 | 1 |
| 30° | 0.5 | 0.866 |
| 45° | 0.707 | 0.707 |
| 60° | 0.866 | 0.5 |

That is why `θ = atan2(ax, az)`. The function gives radians; to convert to degrees you multiply by 57.2958. It matters that the first argument is `ax`: it is the one proportional to sin θ.

When the raw number is converted to g, it is divided by 16384. If you divide two integers, the result has no fraction, so you write `16384.0`.

### Gyroscope: it measures speed
The gyroscope gives the **rotation speed**, in degrees per second. To find the angle you need to add up the speed over time (you will do this in the next lesson). The scale depends on the range: in the ±250 °/s range the raw number is divided by 131, in the ±500 °/s range by 65.5. If you changed the range, you must change the scale too; otherwise the speed is halved or doubled.

Why does the range matter? A falling robot rotates fast. If the speed goes past the range, the value gets stuck at the limit (saturates) and the measurement is wrong. That is why we chose ±500 °/s for the robot in the simulation.

### Bias
A real gyroscope reads a small constant value even when it is still. When this **bias** is added up over time, it turns into a large angle error: a 2 °/s bias means an error of about 40° in 20 seconds. The cure is to take the average of hundreds of measurements at start-up while the robot is still, store it as the bias and subtract it from every later reading.

```
float hiz = gyHam / 65.5 - sapma;
```

### Summary
The accelerometer gives the tilt by looking at gravity with `atan2(ax, az)`; it is disturbed by vibration and acceleration. The gyroscope gives speed, not angle; its scale must match the range, and its bias must be measured and subtracted at start-up. The two have different weaknesses; in the next lesson you will combine them.
