### Three ways to tune
Instead of trying the three numbers at random, you need a method. The first way is **tuning by hand**; the order matters. First, with `Ki = Kd = 0`, increase Kp until it follows the target quickly and overshoots slightly. Then add D and damp the overshoot. Leave I until last, and add it if an offset remains. At each step change one thing only and measure.

### Ziegler-Nichols
In the **Ziegler-Nichols (Z-N)** method you set `Ki = Kd = 0` and increase Kp until the system oscillates permanently. The gain at which it starts to oscillate is **Ku**, and the period of the oscillation is **Pu**. PI tuning:

```
Kc = 0.45 x Ku
Ti = Pu / 1.2
```

In the simulation Ku = 20 and Pu = 0.22 s; this gives Kc = 9 and Ti = 0.183 s. The method works, but to find Ku you have to deliberately bring the system to the stability limit. On a fast vehicle or a fragile mechanism this can cause damage.

### SIMC
The **SIMC** method gets to know the system from a single step test done safely. The system is modelled with these three numbers:
- **k:** gain; when the input increases by 1 unit, how many units the output eventually increases by
- **tau (τ):** time constant; how slowly the response settles
- **theta (θ):** delay; the time after the command during which the output does not move

Formula:

```
Kc = (1 / k) x tau / (tauc + theta)
Ti = min(tau, 4 x (tauc + theta))
```

`tauc` is the time constant you want from the closed loop. A small `tauc` means fast but harsh, a large `tauc` means gentle. As a fast and robust choice, `tauc = theta` is used.

### k, tau and theta from the step response
The motor is given a sudden jump in PWM and the speed is recorded. If the response is first order with delay, the moment the speed reaches 28.3% of its total increase is `t28 = theta + tau / 3`, and the moment it reaches 63.2% is `t63 = theta + tau`. From these:

```
k     = increase in speed / increase in PWM
tau   = 1.5 x (t63 - t28)
theta = t63 - tau
```

In digital control, sampling also counts as delay: on average half a period, that is `DT / 2`, is added to theta. In the simulation k = 0.156, tau = 77 ms and theta = 28 + 10 = 38 ms; with `tauc = theta`, Kc = 6.49 and Ti = 0.077 s were found. Kc is about a third of Ku (20); in other words, it stays far from the stability limit.

### Summary
When tuning by hand, the order is P, D, I. Z-N finds Ku and Pu by making the system oscillate, and can be risky. SIMC, on the other hand, works out k, tau and theta from a single step test and gives Kc and Ti; `tauc` is the single knob between speed and gentleness. Do the measurements while the robot carries its real load and the battery is full.
